Do other methods agree?

The same monitors and filters, judged by six common ways of finding the best buy. When most of them land on the same monitor, the choice is robust; when they disagree, the choice depends on how you trade money for quality.

Glossary
Pareto frontier
The monitors no other monitor beats on both price and rating. Anything off it is a worse buy than something on it.
Convex hull
The frontier monitors a string stretched over the top of the frontier would touch (price on a log scale). They buy rating at the best rate; frontier monitors below the string cost more per rating point.
Knee
The hull monitor where the rating bought per krone drops the most. Past it, spending more buys little or nothing.
Uncertainty (σ)
How far a rating could reasonably be off: about 0.1 for real RTINGS scores, about 0.2 for scores from RTINGS verdict words, 0.45 for monitors rated from two or more outside reviews and 0.6 from a single review. Shown as grey bars on the chart.
Noisy reruns
The whole analysis repeated 300 times with every rating and price nudged within its uncertainty. A pick that wins most reruns is robust; one that wins under 40% is shown as “no clear best value”.
Willingness to pay
What 0.1 more rating is worth to you in kroner. Your pick maximises rating minus price ÷ (10 × that amount).
Hedonic regression
A trend line of rating against price across all monitors. A monitor far above the line “beats its price class”: it rates higher than monitors at its price usually do.
TOPSIS
A multi-criteria method: each monitor is scored by how close it is to an imaginary ideal (cheapest and best) and how far from the worst, with your chosen weights on price and rating.
Satisficing
Decide the minimum rating you need, then buy the cheapest monitor that reaches it.
DEA
Data envelopment analysis: finds the set of “efficient” monitors that no mix of other monitors beats on price and rating. It returns a set, not a single winner.
Calibration
Each outside source's scores are rescaled to the RTINGS scale using the monitors both reviewed, and weighted by how well they agree with RTINGS.
Recency weight
Outside reviews count fully for three years, then lose half their weight every two years. The review-age filter can drop old reviews entirely.

Pareto frontier and convex hull, explained

Pareto frontier (solid staircase). A monitor is on the frontier when no other monitor is both cheaper and better rated. A, B, C and D qualify. E is not: B costs less and rates higher. F is not: D costs less and rates higher. Everything off the frontier is a worse buy than something on it, whatever you care about.

Convex hull (dashed line). Stretch a string over the top of the frontier: the monitors it touches are on the hull. A and D are; B and C sit below the line between A and D. Moving from A to D buys rating at a steady rate, while stopping at B or C means paying more per rating point than going all the way to D. The page draws the hull with price on a log scale and with each rating lowered by its uncertainty.

Best value (ring). The point on the hull where the rating bought per krone drops the most. Past D the hull is flat (nothing rates higher), so D is the best value: spending more buys nothing, spending less gives up a lot of rating per krone saved.

How the rating is built

  1. RTINGS now shows numeric scores to members only. For monitors the real scores come from RTINGS's own pages archived by the Wayback Machine while they were public (test bench v2.1, late 2025 to early 2026).
  2. For the rest, the verdict word for each use (“excellent for office work”) is converted with a scale fitted on the archived monitors:
  3. RTINGS rating = weighted mean of PC gaming, console gaming, office and editing, using the sliders above. The default (1, 0, 1, 1) matches the old “Mixed usage” score.
  4. Outside scores (stars, /10, /100, and PriceRunner user ratings with at least 5 reviews) go on a 0–10 scale and are matched to the RTINGS scale by mean and spread on the monitors both reviewed. Weight = r × n/(n + 10), where r is the correlation with RTINGS and n the shared monitors; 0 if r is negative or n is below 5. All of this is recomputed live when you move the sliders.
  5. Monitors RTINGS has not tested (hollow points) are included when at least two outside sources reviewed them by default (set 1–3 in the controls; with 1, a single review decides the rating, so treat those monitors with care). Their rating is a regression prediction of the RTINGS score from each source: the source's deviation from its average is multiplied by its correlation r, so a single glowing review is pulled back toward the average instead of being taken at face value. These ratings are less certain and get more noise in the shortlist simulation.

How the frontier and shortlist are found

  • Frontier: sort by price; a monitor is on the frontier if it is rated higher than every cheaper monitor. The shortlist table shows each step's cost in kroner per +0.1 rating.
  • Convex hull: of the frontier monitors, those on the upper convex hull of rating against log price. A frontier monitor below the straight line between two hull monitors gives less rating per krone than its neighbours.
  • Uncertainty: the hull and the best value use each rating minus its uncertainty (1σ), so a monitor rated from only a few reviews needs a clear lead to be chosen.
  • Best value: the hull monitor where the rating bought per krone drops the most. After the best monitor nothing rates higher, so that drop counts too and the top of the range can be the best value. This replaces the earlier fitted-curve knee, which could never choose the top monitor.
  • Your pick: the monitor with the highest rating minus price ÷ (10 × what +0.1 rating is worth to you), from the slider. A low setting favours cheap monitors, a high one favours the best.
  • Budget pick and step down: the cheapest frontier monitor, and the best-rated frontier monitor that costs at least 15% less than the best value.
  • Robustness: everything is recomputed times with random noise added to every rating, sized to its uncertainty (), and every price drawn between its cheapest and its third-cheapest in-stock offer (±15% for single-shop prices). If the best value wins fewer than 40% of these reruns, the page says there is no clear best value and lists the candidates.
  • “Stays the best value up to”: the best value's price is raised in 2% steps until another monitor takes over. The slider note lists the settings where your pick changes, and the row below the cards shows the best value for each use preset.
  • Review age: the “Newest review” column shows the latest review behind each rating. “Ignore outside reviews older than” drops older outside reviews from both the calibration and the ratings. RTINGS retests every monitor on its current test bench, so RTINGS scores are always kept (its first review date is shown); PriceRunner user ratings are a running average and are also kept.
  • Other methods: the comparison table runs rating per krone, a hedonic-style price trend, TOPSIS, a satisficing rule and DEA on the same monitors, as a check on the best value.
  • Price trend: after a week of history the cards show the 7-day change, and a price more than 10% below its 30-day median is flagged as a possible short campaign.

Prices: the default is the cheapest in-stock offer including shipping, from each shop's PriceRunner.dk offer; CS MEGASTORE's own-site price (in stock or remote stock, outlet items excluded) gets the shipping cost of its PriceRunner offer. “Lowest item price” ignores shipping. Reviews count fully for three years after publication and then lose half their weight every two years. Marketplace sellers (CDON, eBay, Amazon and similar) are excluded from the lowest price. Every refresh adds a row per monitor to the price history.

Automatic checks: a shop price more than 25% below the median shop is set aside as a likely error until confirmed by hand; review titles that contradict a monitor's size, panel or resolution are rejected; listings with a regional or variant code, monitors sold by fewer than three shops and hand-corrected specs are noted. All findings are listed under “Data issues”. Price alerts appear at the top when the lowest price falls 10% or more since the previous refresh, or reaches its lowest level after at least a week of history.
